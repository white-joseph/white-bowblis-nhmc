\begin{abstract}
\noindent  \\  A reported skilled nursing facility ownership change need not represent a change in who manages the facility. We combine transfer dates from Medicare cost reports with monthly records of owners and ownership shares to identify substantive ownership transitions. Among 6,869 U.S. skilled nursing facilities observed from 2017 to 2024, 1,413 meet our transfer definition. Following a transfer, hours per resident day fall for registered nurses and certified nursing assistants but change little for licensed practical nurses. Total nursing hours change little as the resident census grows, although registered nurse hours fall even in absolute terms. Facilities also become fuller, the Medicare share of resident days rises, the Medicaid share falls, and stays shorten. Measured quality does not broadly worsen. These findings suggest that ownership change alters both nursing skill mix and whom facilities serve; the decline in staffing per resident is one part of a broader change in how facilities operate.

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

In the healthcare sector, ownership is associated with operational decisions that affect patient outcomes. Prior work has focused on ownership as measured by profit status, mainly whether for-profit and not-for-profit providers differ in staffing, costs, and quality of care \citep{grabowski_stevenson_2008, eggleston_et_al_2008, comondore_et_al_2009, holmes_1996}. While this work gives valuable information about profit status, less is known about what happens when a provider changes owners and its location, certification, and payment rules stay in place. Providers change hands for many reasons, including financial distress, succession, and strategic acquisition. Regardless of the reason, new owners control operating decisions that can affect staffing, which patients are admitted, which services are provided, and ultimately the quality of care \citep{bowblis_2011, lu_lu_2022}. 
Since reasons for the ownership change can vary, this will have different implications for operational decisions. Thus, the effect is theoretically ambiguous and ultimately an empirical question.

Skilled nursing facilities (SNFs) are an ideal setting to address this question. An SNF is a healthcare facility that provides skilled nursing care or rehabilitative services and participates in Medicare, Medicaid, or both \citep{eCFR42CFR476.1}. Ownership changes among SNFs are common \citep{prusynski_humbert_mroz_2023, OliveiraBlanco2024}\footnote{Of the roughly 14,000 facilities in our data, 32 percent report at least one ownership change in their Medicare cost reports between January 2017 and June 2024.} and have drawn growing policy and public concern \citep{Khan2024PrivateCapital,MurphyScanlon2026, AFREF2026PEPolling, HealthcareDive2025PEReport}. Furthermore, operational changes made by a new owner could have significant impact on resident quality. For example, nursing staff labor is one of the largest expenses of an SNF \citep{hawk_et_al_2022} but is also associated with quality \citep{bowblis_brunt_2025, lin_2014, zhang_grabowski_2004, harrington_et_al_2000}. If the new owners of an SNF decide to reduce nursing staff levels in response to financial distress, this may put the facility on a better financial footing but could come at the cost of worse resident outcomes.

Existing evidence on ownership change is mixed, in part because most studies examine one type of change, such as private equity acquisitions \citep{GuptaEtAl2024, NBERw34306, huang_bowblis_2019}, chain mergers \citep{Hirth2019EffectsOfChains, Grabowski2016LowQualityNH}, or profit-status conversions \citep{grabowski_stevenson_2008, lu_lu_2022}. Facilities that change owners tend to be financially distressed, less full, more Medicaid-dependent, and lower-staffed \citep{bowblis_2011, lu_lu_2022, prusynski_humbert_mroz_2023}. After a change, RN staffing often falls \citep{lu_lu_2022, GuptaEtAl2024}, while evidence on quality is mixed: private equity acquisitions are linked to higher mortality \citep{GuptaEtAl2024}, but profit-status conversions show little change in quality \citep{grabowski_stevenson_2008}. The studies closest to ours examine ownership changes broadly and find that nurse staffing per resident falls, most sharply for RNs, while the census grows \citep{RyskinaEtAl2024, prusynski_et_al_2025}.

In this paper, we study ownership changes in U.S. SNFs from January 2017 through June 2024 and estimate their effect on operational decisions, including nursing staff, resident census, payer-mix, occupancy, length of stay, and quality. We find that nursing staff levels, measured in hours per resident day, decline for RNs, certified nursing assistants (CNAs), and in total, and are unchanged for licensed practical nurses (LPNs). Measured in raw hours, only RN hours decline; LPN hours rise, and CNA and total nursing hours do not significantly change, so facilities are spreading comparable labor across a larger census. We also find that occupancy rates rise, the Medicare share of patient days rises, the Medicaid share falls mainly at independent facilities, and length of stay shortens. Across quality measures, we see little evidence of broad worsening, with most measures remaining unchanged. In the few cases where there are statistically significant changes, the results are mixed.

This paper differs from and contributes to this literature in three ways. First, we measure ownership change in a more nuanced way than previous studies. A reported change in ownership may not be a substantive change in who manages the SNF. Changes in legal entities or reporting arrangements can appear as ownership changes while those effectively making decisions have not changed. Studies generally rely on one administrative source of reported changes, each of which can misclassify transfers when used alone, or study a particular form of ownership change that is easier to observe. We combine reported ownership-change dates from Medicare cost reports with monthly records of owners and ownership shares. A facility is treated only when both sources record a single change compared to a control group where both sources identify no change.

Second, by observing staffing, census, payer-mix, and length of stay together, we show that ownership change is better understood as a reorientation of the facility's operating model than as a reduction in nursing labor. Because the building, certification, and local market are unchanged, these results show how a change in ownership is associated with a change in how care is produced, holding the local set of providers fixed.

Third, our study period is one in which the industry experienced significant external pressures. Occupancy fell during the COVID-19 pandemic, and SNF employment remained below prepandemic levels \citep{MedPAC2026Report}. In October 2019, Medicare replaced its case-mix system with the Patient Driven Payment Model \citep{cms_2018_pdpm}. CMS also finalized national minimum-staffing standards in 2024 that were repealed before they took effect \citep{cms_2024_min_staffing, cms_2025_staffing_repeal}. SNFs also drew growing political attention. Our estimates therefore describe ownership change in an industry in transition, not in a more stable period.

\section{Institutional Background} \label{sec:background}

An SNF, commonly called a nursing home, provides residents with two types of care. Short-term post-acute care consists of high-intensity rehabilitation to help a patient return home after a hospital stay \citep{CMSBenefitManualCh8}. These types of stays are also known as post-acute care stays. Long-term residential care consists of lower-intensity custodial care focused on personal needs and activities of daily living \citep{CMSBenefitManualCh16}. For this group, the SNF becomes the resident's home, and these residents are often referred to as long-stayers.

Post-acute and long-stayers are financed by different sources. Medicare is the primary payer for post-acute care, covering up to 100 days per spell of illness; however, the typical Medicare-covered stay was about 31 days in 2024 \citep{MedPAC2026Report}. Medicaid is the primary payer for long-term residential care, and accounts for the majority of SNF resident days. Some residents initially pay for long-term care themselves, but because of its high cost, most spend down their assets and become eligible for Medicaid early in their stay. Both programs set payment rates administratively, so SNFs cannot negotiate prices, making them price-takers.

Medicare and Medicaid compensate facilities very differently. The aggregate Medicare margin for freestanding SNFs was 24 percent in 2024,\footnote{The twenty-fifth consecutive year it exceeded 10 percent.} while the non-Medicare margin, driven primarily by Medicaid, was negative 2.3 percent \citep{MedPAC2026Report}. Medicare revenue therefore cross-subsidizes Medicaid losses, and facilities with a higher Medicaid share of patient days face correspondingly weaker finances and staffing capacity \citep{bowblis_brunt_2025}. Because prices are fixed, a facility can raise revenue mainly by serving more residents, or by serving residents who pay more (i.e., raising occupancy and admitting more patients covered by Medicare).

Nursing labor is one of a facility's largest expenses \citep{hawk_et_al_2022}. There are three types of nursing staff. RNs, including Directors of Nursing, are responsible for clinical assessment, diagnosis, care planning, and delegation; LPNs are responsible for basic and routine nursing care under the direct supervision of an RN or physician; and CNAs, supervised by licensed nurses, provide most hands-on daily care and are restricted to observation and reporting \citep{ncsbn2021model}. Scope-of-practice rules limit which tasks each type may perform, but facilities have flexibility in the amount of labor they use for each staff type. Each nurse type is paid very differently. In May 2022, mean hourly wages in SNFs were \$37.11 for RNs, \$28.10 for LPNs, and \$16.90 for CNAs \citep{bls_oews_2022}. Thus, an RN hour costs about 2.2 times a CNA hour.

Minimum-staffing standards constrain the number and mix of nursing staff used by SNFs. However, most state standards set a floor on overall staffing levels, not on the mix of staff types \citep{grabowski_bowblis_2023, bowblis_ghattas_2017}.\footnote{CMS finalized a national minimum staffing rule in 2024 with phase-in scheduled to begin after our sample ends \citep{cms_2024_min_staffing}. The rule was repealed in 2025 \citep{cms_2025_staffing_repeal} and never took effect during our sample.} Federal rules require sufficient nursing staff and an RN on duty at least eight consecutive hours a day \citep{eCFR42CFR483.35}. This means SNFs have significant flexibility to change the mix of nursing staff types and still meet minimum state level requirements. Because RNs are the most expensive type, a facility seeking to lower costs has an incentive to use cheaper staff, and facilities facing staffing mandates have substituted toward less expensive nurse types \citep{bowblis_ghattas_2017}. 

Within this context, if an SNF wants to maximize profits --- after an ownership change --- we would expect to see three changes: a higher occupancy rate, a shift toward Medicare residents and therefore shorter stays, and a shift in nursing staff mix toward cheaper types (e.g., fewer RNs). Because we do not observe owners' objectives or decisions directly, we ask whether the changes in operational characteristics (e.g., occupancy rate) after an ownership change are consistent with these predictions. Furthermore, these changes could affect residents. Higher nursing staff levels are associated with higher quality \citep{harrington_et_al_2000, zhang_grabowski_2004}, and the association is concentrated in RN staffing \citep{lin_2014}. Thus, depending on how an SNF’s operational characteristics change, the SNF may have a higher or lower quality.

\section{Measuring Ownership Change} \label{sec:measuring-ownership}

Ownership change is difficult to measure because ownership structures are frequently layered, with an operating entity held by other corporations, trusts, or partnerships. A change in the listed owner of an SNF may not actually correspond to a change in who runs day-to-day operations of a facility. Legal restructuring, transfers among family members, and changes in reporting conventions can all appear as ownership changes in administrative data without any change in who manages the facility.\footnote{For example, state Medicaid supplemental payment frameworks in Texas \citep{tex_admin_code_353_1302}, Utah \citep{utah_admin_code_r414_505_5}, and Indiana \citep{ind_code_12_15_14} allow private SNFs to transfer their licenses to non-state government entities to secure higher reimbursement rates. These are recorded as a change to government ownership in administrative databases, yet the management and operations remain with the original private operators \citep{utah_auditor_2026_snf_upl, kffhn_2017_indiana_nh, kxan_2021_qipp}. For this reason, we exclude government-owned facilities from our sample (Section~\ref{sec:dataandsample}).}

Studies on SNF ownership changes generally use one of three administrative sources, and each, when used alone, can misclassify ownership changes. The Healthcare Cost Report Information System (HCRIS), also known as the Medicare Cost Reports, records whether a facility reported a change in ownership during the reporting period and the effective date of that change. It does not, however, establish that management changed, so some flagged transfers are changes within the same ownership group. The Provider Enrollment, Chain, and Ownership System (PECOS) records changes of ownership processed through Medicare enrollment. Like HCRIS, it can record reported legal changes rather than changes in management, and it dates them by a tie-in notice that can lag the effective date by several months \citep{CMS2022SNFCHOWGuidance}.\footnote{PECOS also releases snapshots at points in time that only identify the most recent ownership changes. Therefore, any studies using PECOS \citep[e.g.,][]{prusynski_et_al_2025, StevensonEtAl2023, OliveiraBlanco2024} can miss ownership changes that may have taken place before the most recently identified change.} The Nursing Home Compare (NHC) Archive reports monthly owner names, roles, types, and shares, making it possible to observe who owns a facility over time. Its detail, however, can record changes in legal entities that generate false positives.\footnote{In our data, there are records where an individual listed by name in one month appears the next month as that person's revocable living trust.}

We adopt a two-step approach to identify substantive ownership changes. Step 1 is to identify reported ownership changes and their dates in HCRIS. Each cost report records the date of any change of ownership during its reporting period, and we treat each distinct reported date from January 2017 onward as one change, dated to that calendar month.\footnote{All dates are converted to calendar months, so changes occurring at the beginning or end of a month are treated identically.} We use HCRIS for the date because its effective date is not subject to the PECOS tie-in lag. A transferred facility may change its name, but nearly all buyers keep the seller's existing CMS Certification Number (CCN) \citep{CMS2024AppendixPP, eCFR42CFR489.18}, so we can follow the same facility across the transfer.

Step 2 is to verify each reported change with the NHC Archive. The NHC Archive lists each facility's owners by role: 5 percent or greater direct ownership interest, 5 percent or greater indirect ownership interest, or partnership interest. NHC records can list owners at several levels, since an operating entity may itself be owned by other entities. For each facility, we use the top level of ownership reported, so that reorganizations among entities lower in the structure are not counted as ownership changes. We use indirect owners when they are reported, then direct owners, then partnership interests. We compare each record with the facility's previous record and classify a change when at least 50 percent of ownership shares change. Where ownership percentages are missing, we normalize the reported shares or assign equal weights across the remaining owners. To avoid flagging restructuring within a family, we do not count a change when at least 80 percent of the ownership stake remains with owners who share a surname.

A facility is considered in the treatment group if both steps identify exactly one change during our study period, and the dates from HCRIS and the NHC Archive fall within six months of each other.\footnote{For treated facilities, we define the ownership-change month as the month in which HCRIS identifies the change.} SNFs in the control group have no ownership changes in either dataset. SNFs where the sources disagree, or that have more than one change, are excluded from our sample. Table~\ref{tab:ownership-classification-sample} classifies SNFs by the number of ownership changes in each source. The additional restrictions described in Section~\ref{sec:dataandsample} are applied to these SNFs to construct our final sample of 6,869 facilities.

Relying on a single source introduces two kinds of error. The first is misclassification of treated or control groups. Some SNFs flagged as changing owners did not, and some that did are not flagged and end up in the control group. If these errors are unrelated to the outcomes, they bias the estimated effect toward zero. In an event study, misclassified controls cause an additional problem, because control facilities pin down the calendar-time effects, so a control facility that actually changed owners pulls those effects toward its own post-change outcomes. The second is misdating the change. A wrong change date places post-change months in the pre-period, which can create spurious pre-trends. Requiring both sources to agree on a single change and its timing limits both errors. The cost is a smaller sample, so our approach trades some statistical power for less bias.

\section{Data and Methods} \label{sec:dataandmethods}

\subsection{Data and Sample} \label{sec:dataandsample}

Our unit of observation is a facility-month for staffing and operating outcomes and a facility-quarter for quality outcomes. We combine three administrative data sources. The Payroll-Based Journal (PBJ) reports daily hours worked by staff type and daily resident census derived from the Minimum Data Set (MDS). We aggregate both to the facility-month level. HCRIS provides occupancy, payer-mix, length of stay, chain affiliation, certified beds, and reported ownership-change dates. Cost reports are filed once per fiscal year, so we assign each report's values to every month or quarter of its reporting year. When a facility's outgoing and incoming cost reports both cover the same calendar month, which occurs around an ownership transfer, we average the reported values for that month. The NHC Archive reports monthly ownership information and quarterly CMS quality measures.

Our sample covers January 2017 through June 2024, classifying ownership changes as described in Section~\ref{sec:measuring-ownership}. We exclude hospital-based facilities,\footnote{Hospital-based SNFs differ from freestanding facilities in their structure and financing. Because they are within hospitals, staffing policies and resource allocation may be jointly determined with hospital operations and respond to hospital shocks and regulations rather than SNF-specific incentives \citep{MedPAC2026Report}.} facilities in Alaska and Hawaii, facilities with fewer than 15 certified beds, and any facility government-owned at any point during the sample.\footnote{We exclude government-owned facilities since transfers to government ownership can reflect supplemental-payment arrangements rather than changes in management (Section~\ref{sec:measuring-ownership}). Government-owned SNFs also operate in a different financial context \citep{MedPAC2026Report}.} We also drop facility-months with implausible staffing using \citet{cms_fivestar_2026} methodology: RN and LPN hours both zero, total hours per resident day below 1.5 or above 12, or nurse aide hours per resident day above 5.25. Our final monthly sample contains 6,869 facilities and 556,747 facility-months; 1,413 facilities experience a verified ownership change. Our quarterly sample covers 6,857 facilities.

\subsection{Outcomes}

We examine three groups of outcomes, following the incentives described in Section~\ref{sec:background}. We examine nursing staff since it proxies for costs. We also examine the resident census, occupancy rate, payer-mix, and length of stay which proxy for revenue. Quality is what comes next, because staffing and admissions decisions can affect residents.

We measure nursing staff for three categories using PBJ job codes as defined by \citet{cms_fivestar_2026}: RNs, including RNs with administrative duties and Directors of Nursing; LPNs, including LPNs with administrative duties; and nurse aides, including certified nursing assistants, aides in training, and medication aides, which we refer to as CNAs. For facility \(i\) in month \(t\), hours per resident day (HPRD) is

\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:hprd}
  \text{HPRD}_{it}
  =
  \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{d \in t} H_{id}}
       {\displaystyle\sum_{d \in t} R_{id}},
\end{equation}
where \(H_{id}\) is the number of hours worked by the staff type in facility \(i\) on day \(d\), and \(R_{id}\) is the number of resident days on that day. Total HPRD is the sum of the three staff categories. We report HPRD in levels and logs. We separately estimate the effects on the numerator and denominator of this ratio. Quarterly staffing measures for the quality specifications sum hours and resident days over the quarter.

Occupancy rate is patient days divided by available bed-days; where available bed-days are not reported, we use certified beds times days in the fiscal year. Payer-mix is the Medicare and Medicaid shares of patient days, and average length of stay is taken directly from HCRIS.

We use MDS-based quality measures from the NHC Archive. We use eight long-stay measures that fall into two groups. The first reflects care routines that can substitute for staff time. These include: catheter use, antipsychotic medication use, and anti-anxiety or hypnotic medication use. The second group covers resident outcomes: pressure injuries, falls with major injury, weight loss, decline in physical functioning, and urinary tract infections. We also estimate effects on two short-stay measures: new antipsychotic medication and improvement in physical function.\footnote{Pressure injuries are estimated on 2018–2023 and improvement in function on 2017–2022, the years in which each is reported.} Lower values are better for all measures except improvement in physical function. Appendix Table X defines each measure.

\subsection{Empirical Strategy}

We employ a staggered event-study design that compares changes within facilities before and after an ownership change with contemporaneous changes among never-treated facilities and a treated facility's own pre-period months. We estimate the following set of event-study regressions,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:event-study}
Y_{it}
=
\sum_{\substack{\tau=-24\\ \tau\notin\{-4,-3,-2,-1\}}}^{24}
\beta_{\tau}\mathbf{1}[T_{it}=\tau]
+
X_{it}\gamma
+
\mu_i
+
\lambda_t
+
\varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
where \(T_{it}\) is months relative to the ownership change, \(X_{it}\) is a vector of controls, \(\mu_i\) and \(\lambda_t\) are facility and calendar-month fixed effects, and event time runs from \(-24\) to \(24\), with the endpoints absorbing all earlier and later periods. In our quality specification, \(t\) indexes quarters, \(\lambda_t\) are calendar-quarter fixed effects, and event time runs from \(-8\) to \(8\), omitting \(\tau=-1\) and \(\tau=0\). We also estimate the following regression,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:twfe-post}
Y_{it}
=
\delta \textit{Post}_{it}
+
X_{it}\gamma
+
\mu_i
+
\lambda_t
+
\varepsilon_{it}.
\end{equation}
to summarize each outcome with a single post-change coefficient, where \(\textit{Post}_{it}\) equals one for facility-months after the ownership change and \(\delta\) is the coefficient of interest. Both regressions are estimated on the same samples. Standard errors are two-way clustered by facility and calendar period. Log specifications drop zero values.

Because two-way fixed-effects estimates can be biased by comparisons with already-treated facilities when effects vary over time \citep{goodman_bacon_2021}, we also estimate a stacked design that compares each treated cohort only to never-treated and not-yet-treated facilities. The stacked estimates have the same signs and statistical significance for RN, CNA, and total HPRD, with magnitudes within 15 percent of our main estimates; the LPN estimate remains small (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:stacked-twfe-post}).

An ownership change unfolds over several months. A buyer and seller agree to a sale, and closing typically waits for state regulatory approval. During this period, the buyer may have in place contractual restrictions which impact how the seller operates until the deal closes. The seller also has an incentive to keep the census and the finances stable. The recorded date is the closing date, and because nursing staff can adjust in a short period of time, the months before closing may already reflect the impact of the ownership change. We therefore exclude the three months before the closing date (\(\tau=-3,-2,-1\)), and use \(\tau=-4\) as the reference period in our monthly sample, and exclude the quarter of the ownership change (\(\tau=0\)) and use \(\tau=-1\) as the reference period in the quarterly sample. This effectively creates a "donut" hole in which any disruption from the transition does not impact our results. This leaves 553,013 facility-months in the monthly estimation sample. Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:wald-test-staffing} and~\ref{tab:wald-test-quality} report joint Wald tests, which support the "donut" hole approach. Our estimates also change little when we widen or narrow the donut, or when we exclude facility-months that have gaps in PBJ reporting (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:twfe-robustness-summary}).  

Ownership changes plausibly affect facility characteristics that would normally be controlled for, such as occupancy, payer-mix, length of stay, acuity, profit status, and chain affiliation. Instead, we do not condition on these characteristics, and treat occupancy, payer-mix, and length of stay as outcomes. The only control we use for all specifications is certified beds, which requires state approval to change and rarely does. For quality outcomes, we report specifications that condition on RN, LPN, and CNA HPRD. Because staffing itself responds to ownership change, these specifications hold fixed one channel through which ownership change may affect quality. They show the association between ownership change and quality at a given level of staffing, not the total effect.

Using each SNF’s chain status at the start of our sample, we examine whether the effects of ownership change differ between chain-affiliated and independent SNFs.\footnote{If unavailable, we define it as the facility’s first observation.} A facility is chain-affiliated if its cost report lists a home office and independent otherwise. We do this because a chain-affiliated facility already operates under policies set by a larger organization, so a new owner may have less to change than at an independent facility, where staffing and admissions decisions are made by the facility’s own owner. Chains are also active buyers and sellers in this market \citep{Grabowski2016LowQualityNH, prusynski_humbert_mroz_2023}. We use baseline status because chain status frequently changes around the ownership change, so the contemporaneous measure is partly a result of treatment. We estimate Equation~\ref{eq:twfe-post} separately for each group and test the difference using a specification that interacts the post dummy, certified beds, and the calendar fixed effects with baseline chain affiliation.

\subsection{Operating Outcomes}

While there is a rise in resident days, that is one part of a broader change in how facilities operate after undergoing an ownership change. Table~\ref{tab:post-business} reports estimates for the operating outcomes. Occupancy rises by 2.7 percentage points. The payer-mix shifts towards Medicare, as its share of patient days increases by 1.6 percentage points (roughly 12 percent of its mean). The Medicaid share falls by 1.2 percentage points, although only significant at the 10 percent level. Average length of stay falls by 13 days (about 8 percent).

These results are consistent with the payment environment described in Section~\ref{sec:background}. Since SNFs are price-takers and those prices are set administratively, the main ways a facility can change its revenues are by increasing occupancy rates and shifting who pays for its residents. Our results are consistent with an increase in Medicare payer-mix. The facility is fuller and residents have shorter stays. Since these outcomes come from annual cost reports, their timing is less precise than the monthly staffing estimates. 

Occupancy rates and the Medicaid payer-mix change mainly at independent facilities. Occupancy rates rise by 3.9 percentage points among independent facilities and by 2.0 among chains. The Medicaid share falls by 3.6 percentage points among independent facilities but does not change among chains. Length of stay falls by 22.8 days among independent facilities, compared with a smaller 8.3 among chains; however, that difference is not statistically significant. The Medicare share rises by a similar amount in both groups (1.5 and 1.8).

\subsection{Quality}

Because nursing staff levels decline after an ownership change, and staffing is associated with quality (Section~\ref{sec:background}), we would expect quality to decline as well. Table~\ref{tab:post-quality} shows that, with the exception of weight loss, it does not. Weight loss rises by 0.21 percentage points, significant only at the 10 percent level. Every other long-stay measure either shows no statistically significant change or improves, and neither short-stay measure changes significantly. Adding RN, LPN, and CNA HPRD as controls (column 2) has little effect on the estimates. Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:quality_routine_process_landscape} and~\ref{fig:quality_resident_outcomes_landscape} show the corresponding event-study estimates.

By chain status, weight loss rises by 0.58 percentage points among independent facilities and is unchanged among chains, a statistically significant difference. Decline in physical functioning rises among chains, but only at the 10 percent level, and the difference between groups is not significant. No other measure worsens in either group.

Overall, measured quality does not broadly worsen after an ownership change. However, these measures are imperfect, and we are not able to observe hospitalization, mortality, or resident experience.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

After an SNF undergoes an ownership change, it operates differently. It fills more of its beds, admits more residents covered by Medicare, discharges them sooner, and spreads a similar number of nursing hours across a larger census. Only the most expensive labor decreases: RN hours fall while LPN hours rise. Each of these changes matches a prediction from Section~\ref{sec:background}. Because prices are set administratively, fuller beds and a larger share of residents paid for by Medicare are the main ways a facility can raise revenue. From the cost side, RNs are the most expensive nursing staff, and minimum-staffing rules leave room to reduce their hours. While we do not observe whether profits rose or what owners intended, these changes make more sense as one strategy than as separate responses.

The change in skill mix stands out even more once we consider who the facility now serves. Post-acute residents need more intensive care than long-stay residents, and clinical assessment and care planning are RN responsibilities \citep{ncsbn2021model}. A facility moving toward Medicare residents would need more RN time per resident, not less. Instead, licensed nursing shifts from RNs to LPNs. LPN hours rise by more than RN hours fall, so the facility keeps its licensed nursing time but changes who provides it. \citet{bowblis_ghattas_2017} document the same substitution toward less expensive nurse types under staffing mandates; however, here it happens without any change in regulation. This suggests the RN decline reflects a change in how clinical work is divided within the facility, not only an effort to cut nursing costs.

Our results can help reconcile findings in prior work that appear to conflict. \citet{RyskinaEtAl2024} find that after an ownership change, Five-Star staffing and inspection ratings decline while the quality-measure rating improves. If facilities were cutting staff, an increase in measured quality would be surprising. Our staffing decomposition helps clarify this. The staffing rating is built largely from case-mix-adjusted hours per resident day \citep{cms_fivestar_2026}, so a facility that admits more residents without adding staff sees its rating fall even though its nursing hours have not decreased. The quality measures we observe hold steady or improve. A falling staffing rating and a rising quality rating are therefore both what we would expect from a facility that grows its census with a similar workforce. \citet{prusynski_et_al_2025} also find that staffing per resident falls as the census grows, and our decomposition shows how much of that decline the census accounts for. On RNs, our results agree with prior work. RN cuts follow nonprofit-to-for-profit conversions \citep{lu_lu_2022} and private equity acquisitions \citep{GuptaEtAl2024}. We find this holds across a broad set of verified transfers, not just one type of buyer, which suggests the RN decline is a common feature of ownership change rather than something specific to one form of ownership.

The difference between fewer nursing staff and more residents matters for how staffing is monitored. The Five-Star staffing rating and the 2024 federal minimum-staffing standards \citep{cms_2024_min_staffing} both measure staffing in hours per resident day. Using that measure, a facility that cuts nurses looks the same as one that admits more residents with the same nurses, even though their labor differs. Ownership changes among SNFs have drawn concern about cost-cutting, so for facilities that have recently changed owners, reporting hours and census separately would show which of the two is happening. Our evidence points to RN hours as the margin that deserves attention. They are the only category that falls outright, and the one most closely linked to quality \citep{lin_2014}.

The differences by chain affiliation suggest that how much a facility changes depends on where it starts. At independent facilities, occupancy rises more, the Medicaid share falls, and total and CNA staffing per resident fall more. The Medicare shift and the RN decline are similar in both groups. One interpretation is that chain-affiliated facilities already follow practices set by a larger organization, so a new owner has less to change. An independent facility has more room to fill its beds and reduce its reliance on Medicaid. The RN decline, however, does not depend on where the facility starts.

Our results for quality speak mostly to long-stay residents, and the reorientation changes who those residents are. When a facility shifts toward short Medicare stays and away from Medicaid, fewer residents stay long enough to be included in the long-stay measurements, and those who do may differ from the long-stay population before the change. That measured quality is largely unchanged despite the RN cuts may reflect licensed nursing time being maintained through LPNs. However, the group that grows as a share of the facility, post-acute Medicare patients, is covered by only two measures. The outcomes that are relevant to them, such as rehospitalization and successful discharge home, are not in our data. Given the RN decline and the link between RN staffing and quality, future work should look first at the post-acute population.

Our measurement approach shapes what these estimates represent. Because we require two sources to agree on a single change, our treated and control groups are cleaner than either source would give alone. The cost is that facilities with repeated or conflicting ownership records are excluded. These facilities are common and are where ownership is hardest to track, so their operating responses may differ from those we estimate. Our approach separates them from substantive single transfers, and studying them directly is a natural extension.

Several other limitations remain. Ownership changes are not randomly assigned. Financially distressed facilities are more likely to be sold \citep{bowblis_2011, lu_lu_2022}. Over the full two years before the change, parallel trends does not hold for RN and LPN HPRD, so we interpret the estimates as associations. Our estimates average over transfers made for different reasons, including distress, succession, and strategic acquisition, and cannot distinguish between them. The recorded transfer dates are closing dates, and the operating-model outcomes come from annual cost reports, so their timing is less precise than that of the staffing outcomes. Finally, the study period includes the COVID-19 pandemic and the introduction of the Patient Driven Payment Model. Calendar fixed effects absorb shocks common to all facilities, but not differences in how facilities were exposed.

\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

SNFs that change owners remain in the same location, with the same certification, and governed by the same Medicare and Medicaid payment rules. What does change is who makes operating decisions within the facility. Ownership records can identify changes that do not change who runs the facility, so we verify HCRIS ownership changes against NHC Archive ownership records. We identify 1,413 SNFs that change ownership a single time from 2017 to 2024 out of a sample of 6,869 facilities.

We find, after an ownership change, HPRD falls for RNs, CNAs, and in total, mostly because the resident census rises. In absolute terms, RN hours fall while LPN hours rise. Facilities also fill more beds, serve a larger share of residents paid for by Medicare, and shorten resident stays. We also find that measured quality does not broadly worsen.

Two things can be taken away from our results. First, any future studies of ownership changes in SNFs should attempt to separate legal or administrative changes from substantive changes in who manages the facility. The verification approach we use applies whenever ownership is layered and the reported changes are noisy. Second, staffing measured in hours per resident day can sometimes obscure changes in labor composition. Staffing in HPRD as well as raw hours should be looked at separately alongside resident census. This is especially true for RNs.

Our results show that facilities serve a larger share of residents paid for by Medicare, and subsequently more post-acute residents; however, our evidence says little about these residents. Future work should examine outcomes that are pertinent to these residents such as mortality, rehospitalization, or discharge rates. Future work should also examine whether different types of ownership changes, such as private equity acquisition or succession, have different operating responses.
